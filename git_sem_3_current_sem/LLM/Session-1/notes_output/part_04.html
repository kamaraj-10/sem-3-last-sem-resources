Word structure and subword models: why word tokens fail

[50:41 – 52:51]
The concept

Common words get their own token. Rare, misspelt or new words are split into smaller pieces.

Word typeWordVocabulary mapping (slide)Tokens
Commonhathat1
Commonlearnlearn1
Variationtaaaaastytaa## aaa## sty3
Misspellinglaernla## ern##2
Novel itemTransformerifyTransformer## ify2
Worst case: a word splits into as many tokens as it has characters (or bytes). Each token gets its own ID and its own embedding.
Intuition + layman analogy

Lego bricks vs pre-built models. Word-level tokens are pre-built models: you must stock a model for every word, and a new word has none. Subwords are bricks: any new word is assembled from bricks you already own.

Embedded-dev lens

Like building a lookup table with an "unknown" slot versus building values from bit-fields. Subwords are the bit-fields.

Justification
  • "taaaaasty" is not a legitimate English word, yet its meaning is still tasty. Subwords keep that meaning visible.
  • A misspelling in word-level tokenization becomes a new vocabulary entry. In subword tokenization it is just split into small pieces.
more tokens per word (longer sequences) vs a smaller, more robust vocabulary.
Professor emphasized
  • We care about the semantic meaning of tokens, so each token needs its own embedding.
  • You should pre-process and correct spellings in the corpus anyway, but residual errors will remain.
Slide notation note: ## marks a continuation in WordPiece (BERT). Byte-level BPE (GPT, Llama, Qwen) marks a leading space instead, e.g. "Ġtasty". "Ohh. Individual entry" = an individual entry. "Transformerify" is an invented word used as an example.
Subword tokenization breaks unfamiliar words into known pieces, so no word is ever unrepresentable.

Types of tokens: word, subword, character, byte

[52:54 – 56:41]
The concept

Same text, four ways to cut it: "unhappiness!"

TypeTokens for "unhappiness!"CountVocabulary size
Wordunhappiness | !2huge (every word form)
Subwordun | happi | ness | !4moderate (30k–100k+)
Characteru n h a p p i n e s s !12small, not popular
ByteUTF-8 bytes12 (ASCII)256

Word tokens (e.g. word2vec)

Cannot handle words unseen in training (OOV). Near-duplicates (apology, apologize, apologist) waste vocabulary.

Subword tokens

New words split into known pieces. Used by every modern LLM.

Byte tokens

Vocabulary = 256 UTF-8 bytes, so nothing is ever OOV, but sequences become much longer. Byte-level model: ByT5. CANINE is character-level.

Byte-level BPE

Subword merges built on top of bytes. Used by Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, gpt-oss.

Justification
  • Small vocabulary means a smaller embedding matrix and no OOV problem (byte level).
  • Word-level: more token IDs, more embeddings, a bigger input matrix, more memory and weaker generalisation, because apology/apologize/apologist share one meaning but have separate tokens.
  • Byte-level: sequences are much longer, so attention costs more.
vocabulary size vs sequence length. Subword sits in the middle, which is why it won.
Common mistakes
  • Assuming one character = one byte. True for English (ASCII) only. Hindi and many scripts need 3 bytes.
  • Saying byte-level tokenization has 256 tokens per word. The vocabulary has 256 entries, and a word is a sequence of them.
Professor emphasized
  • If an OOV word appears at inference time, "your application will fail" to produce a proper answer.
  • Word-based tokenization is how classical static embeddings (word2vec) worked, and is "not popular" now.
  • Character-based tokens are "used in some research models".
She says "Unicode, 0 to 255 bytes". The slide says UTF-8 bytes, which is the encoding actually used. "word to work" → word2vec. Board: "out of vocab", "Unicode", "0–255 bytes", "3 bytes, 3 tokens".

Word tokens have OOV problems, byte tokens make long sequences, subword tokens balance both.

Every tokenizer has two parts

Slide "Subword tokenization". IDs [12, 5] are illustrative values on the slide.
Intuition + layman analogy

Compiling vs running. Training the tokenizer is like compiling a dictionary of abbreviations from a big library. Using it is a clerk applying that dictionary to every new letter. The clerk must use the same dictionary every time.

Embedded-dev lens

Learner is the build step that produces a lookup table and rules into flash. Segmenter is the run-time code that only reads them.

Justification
  • Frequent words get their own tokens, rare words split into subwords: the vocabulary is "built dynamically" from data.
  • Training runs once. Inference only applies the saved rules.
the model is trained with one particular tokenizer, so at inference you must use that same tokenizer. A mismatch gives wrong IDs and nonsense.
Common mistakes
  • Using a different tokenizer for inference than the one used for training or for the model you downloaded.
  • Thinking the tokenizer learns during inference. Vocabulary and merge rules are fixed after tokenizer training.
Professor emphasized
  • Every model publisher trains their own tokenizer, tokenizes their corpus, and then trains the Transformer. Tokenizer training is a separate step.
  • A vocabulary may have 50,000 or even a million entries (she says "1 million" loosely) .
  • BPE will be explained in detail next.
"Unigram Language Modelling" = Unigram LM tokenization (Kudo). "become popular using BERT" is unclear. The slide notation note says WordPiece is BERT's tokenizer . "by pair algorithm" → byte pair algorithm. "BP algorithm" → BPE.
Extra reading
  • Sennrich, Haddow and Birch (2016): Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units: introduces BPE for open-vocabulary NMT. Fetched and checked.
Tokenizer = learner (trains vocab and merge rules once) + segmenter (applies them to every input). Always reuse the training tokenizer.

The Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) algorithm

[1:00:31 – 1:03:01]
The concept

Start from characters. Repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, until you reach the target vocabulary size.

  1. Pre-tokenize: split the corpus into words with a rule-based splitter (board: "whitespace", or a regular expression).
  2. Word dictionary with frequency counts: ("hug", 10), ("pug", 5), ("pun", 12), ("bun", 4), ("hugs", 5).
  3. Start from single characters: the initial vocabulary is all unique characters. Count adjacent pairs.
  4. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token.
  5. Repeat steps 3–4 until the target vocabulary size.
Initial vocabulary here = 7 unique characters: b, g, h, n, p, s, u.
Intuition + layman analogy

Text-message shorthand. If "u" and "g" keep appearing side by side, people invent a single shorthand "ug". Then "hug" is easier to write as h+ug. Shorthands that save the most typing are adopted first.

Embedded-dev lens

Same idea as dictionary compression (LZ-style, or Huffman by frequency): common patterns get short codes first.

Pair score (BPE)
count(a, b) = wordsΣw freq(w) × occurrences of the pair (a, b) in w
  • (a, b) two adjacent symbols in the current segmentation
  • freq(w) number of times the word appears in the corpus
  • The pair with the highest count is merged. Ties need a fixed rule

Formula is a restatement of the professor's "weighted by word frequency" rule. notation added

Common mistakes
  • Counting pairs per distinct word and forgetting to multiply by the word frequency.
  • Merging pairs that span a word boundary. Pre-tokenization keeps pairs inside words.
  • Forgetting to recount pairs after every merge, since the segmentation changed.
Professor emphasized
  • The vocabulary size is chosen by the tokenizer trainer, e.g. 30,000 or 50,000. Training stops when it is reached.
  • The toy example is shown for four iterations.
"BGH Nsp" → b, g, h, n, p, s, u. "U&G" → (u, g). Board: "1 2 3 4 5 6 7" counts the 7 initial characters, "initial vocab = unique characters".
BPE: count adjacent pairs weighted by word frequency, merge the winner, repeat until the vocabulary is the size you want.

BPE worked iterations, the segmenter, and the "mug" problem

[1:03:06 – 1:05:43]
The concept
IterCurrent vocabularyWords after the merge
1b g h n p s u(h u g, 10), (p u g, 5), (p u n, 12), (b u n, 4), (h u g s, 5)
2… + ug(h ug, 10), (p ug, 5), (p u n, 12), (b u n, 4), (h ug s, 5)
3… + un(h ug, 10), (p ug, 5), (p un, 12), (b un, 4), (h ug s, 5)

Segmenter: apply the merges in the learned order. bug → b u g → b ug. hugs → h u g s → h ug s → hug s.
Scenario

What is merged in rounds 2, 3 and 4?

After mergePair counts that matter
Round 2 (after "ug")(u, n) = 12 + 4; (p, u) = 12; (h, ug) = 15; (ug, s) = 5; (p, ug) = 5; (b, u) = 4
Round 3 (after "un")(h, ug) = 10 + 5; (p, un) = 12; (b, un) = 4; (p, ug) = 5; (ug, s) = 5
Step-by-step computation
1
Round 2 winner. (u, n) = 12 + 4 = 16, which beats (h, ug) = 15 and (p, u) = 12. merge → "un"
2
Round 3 winner. (h, ug) = 10 (hug) + 5 (hugs) = 15, which beats (p, un) = 12. merge → "hug"
3
Count the vocabulary. 7 base + 3 merges (ug, un, hug) = 10 tokens
4
Segment "bug". b u g → apply ug → b ug (no un, no hug)
5
Segment "hugs". h u g s → h ug s → hug s = 2 tokens
10 tokens in vocabularyhugs → [hug, s]vocabulary size = base characters + number of merges
Code (toy BPE learner and segmenter) 
from collections import Counter
words = {("h","u","g"):10, ("p","u","g"):5, ("p","u","n"):12,
         ("b","u","n"):4,  ("h","u","g","s"):5}
merges = []
for _ in range(3):
    pairs = Counter()
    for w, f in words.items():
        for a, b in zip(w, w[1:]): pairs[(a, b)] += f   # weighted by word frequency
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get); merges.append(best)
    new = {}
    for w, f in words.items():
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(w):
            if i < len(w)-1 and (w[i], w[i+1]) == best: out.append(w[i]+w[i+1]); i += 2
            else: out.append(w[i]); i += 1
        new[tuple(out)] = f
    words = new
print(merges)   # [('u','g'), ('u','n'), ('h','ug')]
Justification: the "mug" problem
  • Frequent pieces (hug, un, ug) become single tokens, so common words are short sequences.
  • "mug" fails: the corpus never had "m", so "m" is not in the vocabulary and the segmenter outputs [UNK].
Fix (slide): start from the 256 bytes, not characters → byte-level BPE. Every string is then representable with no OOV. This is the continuation in the next part.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking BPE vocabulary only contains whole words. It mixes characters, subwords and whole words.
  • Believing character-level BPE solves OOV. It only works if the character was seen in the corpus. "m" was not.
Professor emphasized
  • "UG has come 20 times, 10 plus 5 plus 5."
  • Training continues until the vocabulary hits the size you set (e.g. 50,000). "That's how every model designer and publisher builds their vocabularies."
  • Board: "hug = 15", "10 tokens in vocab". She raises the example "mug" at the end, which continues in part 05.
"UN, 12 plus 14, 16 times" → 12 + 4 = 16 (slip of the tongue). "Hug, hug five times" refers to hugs (5).

Merging ug, un, hug gives 7 + 3 = 10 tokens. Unseen characters (like "m") produce [UNK], so modern tokenizers start from bytes.

🧠 Quick recall

1. Why do modern LLMs use subword tokens instead of word tokens?
Word tokens cannot handle unseen words (OOV) and waste vocabulary on near-duplicates (apology, apologize). Subwords build any new word from known pieces and keep a manageable vocabulary.
2. How many tokens does the word "Apple" give at byte level, and what is the byte vocabulary size?
5 tokens (65 112 112 108 101). Vocabulary = 256. A Hindi character takes 3 bytes, so 3 tokens.
3. Corpus: ("low", 5), ("lot", 3), ("lows", 2). Which pair merges first?
(l, o): 5 + 3 + 2 = 10. (o, w): 5 + 2 = 7. (o, t): 3. (w, s): 2. Merge (l, o) → "lo".
4. A BPE tokenizer starts with 7 base characters and performs 3 merges. What is the vocabulary size?
10 tokens (7 + 3). Real GPT-2: 256 + 50,000 + 1 special = 50,257.
5. Why must the inference tokenizer match the training tokenizer, and how does byte-level BPE avoid [UNK]?
The model learned embeddings for specific token IDs, so a different tokenizer gives wrong IDs. Byte-level BPE starts from all 256 bytes, so every string can be encoded and no character is out of vocabulary.
